# batch A: points

## [animism] Animism (prehistoric)
-
  The belief, found in most traditional cultures, that living things and sometimes places are inhabited by spirits. It is often treated as the oldest form of religion.

## [greekgods] Greek gods as archetypes (c. 8th c. BCE)
-
  The Olympian gods each personify a domain of human character: war, love, cunning, craft. They quarrel, scheme and take sides in human affairs, as in Homer.

## [genesis] Genesis and the Hebrew Bible (c. 6th c. BCE)
- facet: Genesis 1 | by: Hebrew Bible
  The opening chapter of the Bible, in which God separates light from darkness and the waters above from those below, then makes plants, animals and humans. It is usually read as an account of the creation of the physical world in six days.
- facet: Adam and Eve | by: Hebrew Bible
  The biblical story of the first humans living in a paradise without suffering until they eat from the tree of knowledge and are expelled.
- facet: Tower of Babel | by: Hebrew Bible
  The story in which humanity's shared language is confused so that people can no longer understand each other and their great tower is abandoned. It appears in Genesis 11 and is traditionally read as a warning against human overreach.
- facet: The God of Abraham
  The Abrahamic religions worship a single God who makes a covenant with a people and gives them law.
- facet: The Bible
  The canonical scripture of Judaism and Christianity, a library of myths, laws, histories and poetry.

## [logos] Logos (c. 500 BCE)
- | by: Heraclitus; Stoics; Gospel of John
  Greek for word, reason or rational order: the principle by which the cosmos is intelligible. The Gospel of John opens with it ('In the beginning was the Word'), carrying the idea into Christianity.

## [buddhism] Buddhism and meditation (5th c. BCE)
- facet: Buddhism | by: Siddhartha Gautama
  A contemplative tradition that studies experience from the inside through meditation, teaching that the self is constructed and that suffering comes from attachment. Advanced practitioners report being able to deconstruct ordinary perception and selfhood.
- facet: The Dalai Lama | by: Tibetan Buddhism
  In Tibetan Buddhism the Dalai Lama is held to be the same being reborn in successive bodies. Each new Dalai Lama is found as a child and raised into the role.
- facet: Michael Taft on enlightenment
  American meditation teacher and author of 'The Mindful Geek', who teaches contemplative practice in secular, non-mystical terms. He hosts the Deconstructing Yourself podcast.

## [plato] Plato: forms and the cave (c. 375 BCE)
- | by: Plato
  Plato held that behind the changing things we perceive there are ideal, timeless forms (the perfect triangle, the ideal table). In mathematics, 'Platonism' is the view that mathematical objects really exist independently of minds.

## [aristotle] Aristotle (c. 350 BCE)
- facet: Aristotle
  Greek philosopher who systematised logic, physics and psychology. He described the soul (psyche) not as a ghostly substance but as the organising form of a living body, with vegetative, animal and rational layers.
- facet: De Anima (On the Soul) | by: Aristotle
  Aristotle's treatise on the soul. It describes three levels: a vegetative soul (growth and nutrition, in plants), a sensitive soul (perception and desire, in animals) and a rational soul (in humans). The soul is the form of a living body and does not survive it.
- facet: The unmoved mover | by: Aristotle
  Aristotle argued that if anything in the universe changes, something must ultimately drive that change without itself being moved. This 'prime mover' is the minimal philosophical notion of a god: no personality, just the reason things keep going.

## [stoicism] Stoicism (3rd c. BCE)
- | by: Zeno of Citium and successors
  A school of philosophy teaching that we suffer from our judgments about events rather than the events themselves, and that we should concern ourselves only with what is in our control.

## [christianity] Christianity (1st c. CE onward)
- facet: The Catholic Church
  The religion built on the life and teaching of Jesus; for most of European history the Church was its unifying institution. Beyond its beliefs, it was an organisation that coordinated millions of people for centuries.
- facet: 1 Corinthians 13 | by: Paul the Apostle
  A New Testament passage saying that knowledge, prophecy and sacrifice are worth nothing without love.
- facet: Christian mind-body dualism
  The traditional Christian teaching that a person has an immaterial soul distinct from the body.
- facet: Protestantism | by: Martin Luther and others
  The branch of Christianity that emerged from the Reformation, stressing the individual's direct relationship to God and personal conscience.

## [aquinas] Thomas Aquinas (13th c.)
- facet: Thomas Aquinas
  Medieval theologian who merged Aristotle with Christian doctrine. He argued for God as the necessary ground of existence and described seven virtues: four practical ones (temperance, justice, prudence, courage) and three theological ones (faith, hope, love).
- facet: The seven virtues | by: Thomas Aquinas (after Plato, Aristotle, St Paul)
  The classical list of prudence, justice, temperance and courage, plus the Christian faith, hope and love.

## [golem] The golem (16th c. legend)
- | by: Jewish folklore
  A clay figure brought to life by a rabbi to serve and protect, which follows instructions literally and can run out of control. The best-known version is the sixteenth-century legend of Rabbi Loew of Prague.

## [descartes] René Descartes (1641)
- facet: Descartes
  French philosopher famous for 'I think, therefore I am' and for dividing reality into extended stuff (res extensa) and thinking stuff (res cogitans), the classic statement of mind-body dualism.
- facet: Meditations on First Philosophy | by: René Descartes
  Descartes doubts everything he can and concludes that he exists as a thinking thing, distinct from his body.
- facet: Mind-body dualism | by: René Descartes
  Descartes divided reality into extended stuff (res extensa) and thinking stuff (res cogitans), which leaves the puzzle of how the two interact. Most later philosophy of mind is an attempt to escape that puzzle.

## [occasionalism] Occasionalism (1674)
- | by: Nicolas Malebranche
  A post-Cartesian doctrine that mind and body never directly interact; God causes the matching events on each side on every occasion.

## [empiricism] Empiricism: Locke and Hume (1690–1748)
- facet: Empiricism vs rationalism
  A long dispute about the source of knowledge: experience of the world (empiricists such as Locke and Hume) or reasoning from first principles (rationalists such as Descartes and Leibniz).
- facet: Locke on consciousness | by: John Locke
  Locke defined consciousness as the perception of what passes in one's own mind, and tied personal identity to this continuity of awareness and memory.
- facet: Hume: proportion belief to evidence | by: David Hume (attributed)
  A basic rule of rational thinking: hold each belief only as strongly as the evidence warrants, and keep every non-refuted possibility open.

## [idealism] Idealism (1710 onward)
-
  The view that reality is fundamentally mental and the physical world is an appearance within mind. Bernardo Kastrup's 'analytic idealism' is a current version.

## [leibniz] Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1714)
- facet: Leibniz
  German polymath who co-invented calculus and dreamed of a universal symbolic language in which disputes could be settled by calculation ('let us calculate').
- facet: Leibniz's mill | by: Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz
  A thought experiment from the Monadology: imagine a thinking machine enlarged to the size of a mill so you can walk inside. You would see only parts pushing each other and nothing that explains perception. Leibniz concluded that mechanism cannot explain mind.

## [lamettrie] La Mettrie: Man a Machine (1747)
- | by: Julien Offray de La Mettrie
  A scandalous Enlightenment pamphlet arguing that humans are entirely physical machines, not different in kind from animals, and that mind is a function of organised matter.

## [enlightenment] The Enlightenment (18th c.)
-
  The 18th-century European movement that put reason, evidence and individual autonomy above religious and traditional authority.

## [kant] Immanuel Kant (1781)
- facet: Kant
  German philosopher who tried to ground morality in reason alone: act only on rules you could will to be universal laws, regardless of culture or consequences.
- facet: Critique of Pure Reason | by: Immanuel Kant
  Kant asks what the mind must supply for experience to be possible at all. His answer is that space, time and basic concepts such as cause are forms the mind brings to experience, so we know the world as it appears to us.

## [hegel] G. W. F. Hegel (1807)
- | by: G. W. F. Hegel
  German idealist who saw history as the process by which Spirit (Geist) comes to know itself. Progress, for him, happens through contradictions that are overcome at a higher level.

## [dubois] Du Bois-Reymond: Ignorabimus (1872)
-
  The physiologist du Bois-Reymond declared that science will never know how matter gives rise to consciousness: 'ignoramus et ignorabimus'.

## [epiphenomenalism] Epiphenomenalism (1874)
-
  The view that conscious experience is a by-product of brain activity with no causal influence on the physical world, like steam from a locomotive.

## [emergence] Emergence (1875)
- | by: G. H. Lewes; British emergentists
  The idea that wholes can have properties their parts lack, as wetness belongs to water but not to a single molecule. The disputed question is whether emergent properties are merely convenient descriptions or genuinely new causes.

## [husserl] Phenomenology (1913)
- | by: Edmund Husserl
  Husserl argued that we never see the world itself, only appearances (phenomena). The mind turns them into meaningful objects (noema) through acts of interpretation (noesis), and philosophy should 'bracket' claims about the outside world (epoché) to study this.

## [wittgenstein] Ludwig Wittgenstein (1921–1953)
- facet: Wittgenstein
  Austrian philosopher. His early work tried to define exactly what can be said in a logical language; his later work argued that meaning comes from how language is used in life.
- facet: Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus | by: Ludwig Wittgenstein
  A short, austere book claiming that the world is the totality of facts, that language mirrors facts through logical form, and that what cannot be said clearly must be passed over in silence. Wittgenstein wrote it largely in the trenches of the First World War and later rejected much of it.
- facet: Philosophical Investigations | by: Ludwig Wittgenstein
  Wittgenstein's later work, which abandons the idea of one exact logical language. Meaning comes from how words are used in many overlapping 'language games'.

## [qualia] Qualia (1929–1982)
- facet: Qualia | by: C. I. Lewis (term)
  The 'what it is like' qualities of experience: the redness of red, the painfulness of pain. Many philosophers think they resist physical explanation.
- facet: Mary's room (Epiphenomenal Qualia) | by: Frank Jackson
  The 'Mary' argument. A scientist who knows all the physical facts about colour but has never seen colour seems to learn something new when she first sees red, which Jackson took to show that physical facts are not all the facts.

## [physicalism] Materialism and physicalism (ancient; modern form 20th c.)
- facet: Physicalism
  The thesis that everything that exists is physical or depends on the physical, with no extra mental or supernatural ingredients. It is the default position in most of contemporary science and analytic philosophy.
- facet: Direct realism
  The philosophical position that we perceive the world itself, roughly as it is, rather than an internal representation of it.

## [functionalism] Functionalism and computationalism (1960s)
- facet: Functionalism | by: Hilary Putnam and others
  Functionalism says a mental state is defined by what it does, not what it is made of. Computationalism adds that what minds do can be described as computation.
- facet: Computationalism, weak and strong
  Weak computationalism says minds are computer programs realised in brains. Strong computationalism (digital physics, pancomputationalism) adds that the universe itself is best described as computation that generates patterns of information.

## [lem] Stanisław Lem (1961–1964)
- facet: Stanisław Lem
  Polish science fiction writer and philosopher of technology. 'Solaris' (1961) is about an alien mind humans cannot understand; 'Golem XIV' (1981) is a set of lectures given by a superhuman computer to humanity.
- facet: Solaris | by: Stanisław Lem
  Novel about scientists orbiting a planet whose ocean is a single vast mind that they can never understand. Stanisław Lem wrote it in 1961; the ocean answers the scientists by materialising figures from their own memories.
- facet: Summa Technologiae | by: Stanisław Lem
  Lem's non-fiction survey of future technologies, including artificial intelligence, virtual reality and directed evolution.

## [dreyfus] Dreyfus: What Computers Still Can't Do (1972 / 1992)
- | by: Hubert Dreyfus
  A philosopher's long-running critique of symbolic AI. Dreyfus argued that human skill rests on bodily know-how and context that cannot be written down as explicit rules.

## [nagel] Nagel: What Is It Like to Be a Bat? (1974)
- | by: Thomas Nagel
  Nagel argues that an organism is conscious if there is something it is like to be that organism, and that objective science seems to leave this point of view out.

## [hofstadter] Hofstadter: strange loops (1979–2007)
- | by: Douglas Hofstadter
  In 'Gödel, Escher, Bach' and 'I Am a Strange Loop', Hofstadter suggests the self arises when a symbol system becomes able to refer to itself, a tangled feedback loop like Gödel's self-referential sentence. On this view 'I' is a pattern, not a thing, and could in principle exist in other substrates.

## [searle] Searle: the Chinese room (1980)
- | by: John Searle
  Berkeley philosopher who held that consciousness is a biological product of the brain's specific causal powers, like digestion, so that a program alone, however clever, cannot have it. His Chinese Room thought experiment is the best-known argument that manipulating symbols is not the same as understanding them.

## [levine] The explanatory gap (1983)
- | by: Joseph Levine
  Levine's name for the gap between any physical or functional description of the brain and an explanation of why it feels the way it does.

## [mysterianism] Mysterianism (1989)
- | by: Colin McGinn; Noam Chomsky
  The view that the human mind is simply not built to understand how consciousness arises from matter, as a dog cannot understand calculus. Its best-known defender is the philosopher Colin McGinn.

## [dennett] Daniel Dennett (1991)
- facet: Daniel Dennett
  American philosopher who argued that consciousness is not an inner theatre but many parallel drafts of interpretation, and that the 'self' is a useful narrative centre. He treated the 'hard problem' as a confusion to be dissolved rather than a mystery to be solved.
- facet: Consciousness Explained | by: Daniel Dennett
  Dennett's book proposing the 'multiple drafts' model: there is no single place or moment where experience happens, only competing processes whose winners shape behaviour and memory.
- facet: Zimbos | by: Daniel Dennett
  A zombie is a being that behaves as if conscious with nobody home. Dennett's 'zimbo' is a zombie with higher-order self-monitoring, so it believes and reports that it is conscious. His 'zank' is a parody: something that works exactly like a bank but supposedly lacks the essence of a bank.

## [embodied] Embodied and enactive cognition (1927–2006)
- facet: Phenomenology and enactivism | by: Martin Heidegger; Maurice Merleau-Ponty; Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, Eleanor Rosch; Alva Noë; Shaun Gallagher
  A line of philosophy that starts from lived experience and holds that mind is constituted by a body acting in a world, not by inner representations alone.
- facet: How the Body Shapes the Way We Think | by: Rolf Pfeifer, Josh Bongard
  A statement of embodied AI: intelligence comes from the interplay of a body, its materials and the world, so robots are needed to study it.
- facet: Sensorimotor theory | by: Kevin O'Regan and Alva Noë
  Experience is not something the brain produces internally; it is a skilled activity of exploring the world, constituted by the lawful links between movement and sensation. It was put forward by Kevin O'Regan and Alva Noë in 2001.

## [egan] Greg Egan (1994)
- | by: Greg Egan
  Hard science fiction about minds that run as software: copies of people living in simulations, citizens who are programs, and what personal identity means when a mind can be copied or merged.

## [chalmers] Chalmers: the hard problem (1995)
- facet: The hard problem of consciousness | by: David Chalmers
  Chalmers separates the 'easy' problems of explaining what the brain does from the 'hard' problem of why any of it is felt. He argues that function alone does not explain experience.
- facet: Philosophical zombies | by: David Chalmers
  A hypothetical creature physically and behaviourally identical to a person but with no inner experience. If such a being is conceivable, the argument goes, consciousness is something over and above physical function.
- facet: David Chalmers
  Philosopher who named the 'hard problem' of consciousness and defends the possibility that experience is a fundamental feature of the world. He has taken the possibility of conscious AI seriously for decades.

## [block] Ned Block: access vs phenomenal consciousness (1995)
- | by: Ned Block
  Philosopher of mind. He raised classic doubts about functionalism ('Troubles with functionalism', 1978) and separated phenomenal consciousness, what it is like, from access consciousness, what is available for report and control (1995).

## [matrix] The Matrix (1999)
- | by: Lana and Lilly Wachowski
  Film in which humans unknowingly live inside a simulated world run by machines. It became the standard popular reference for simulation arguments and for doubting what seems real.

## [simulation] The simulation argument (2003)
- | by: Nick Bostrom
  The suggestion that our universe is a computer simulation run by some more basic civilisation. Nick Bostrom's 2003 argument made it famous: if such simulations are possible and common, we are probably inside one.

## [drescher] Gary Drescher: Good and Real (2006)
- | by: Gary Drescher
  A philosopher-computer scientist's attempt to dissolve paradoxes about time, quantum mechanics, free will and ethics from a strictly mechanistic standpoint. Drescher argues that consciousness, choice and morality all fit inside a deterministic physical universe.

## [watts] Peter Watts: Blindsight (2006–2018)
- | by: Peter Watts
  First-contact novel whose aliens are highly intelligent but not conscious, raising the question of whether consciousness is useful at all. Peter Watts published it in 2006; it is a frequent reference in discussions of machine consciousness.

## [chiang] Ted Chiang (2010)
- | by: Ted Chiang
  Stories about raising artificial minds over many years like children, and about a mechanical being who dissects his own brain to understand thought.

## [her] Her (2013)
- | by: Spike Jonze
  A film in which a lonely man falls in love with an AI operating system that eventually outgrows him.

## [exmachina] Ex Machina (2014)
- | by: Alex Garland
  A film in which a programmer is asked to judge whether a humanoid robot is conscious, and is manipulated by her.

## [westworld] Westworld (2016)
- | by: Jonathan Nolan; Lisa Joy (HBO)
  A television series in which android 'hosts' in a theme park gradually become self-aware, drawing on Julian Jaynes's bicameral mind.

## [thagard] Thagard: energy and substrate independence (2022)
- | by: Paul Thagard
  Thagard argues that because real information processing costs energy, and different materials have very different energy budgets, a mind cannot simply be moved to any substrate; so functionalism is in trouble. The paper appeared in the journal Philosophy of Science.

## [chirimuuta] Mazviita Chirimuuta (2024)
-
  Edinburgh philosopher of neuroscience, author of 'The Brain Abstracted'. She argues that computational models of the brain are useful simplifications and should not be mistaken for what the brain literally is.

## [lerchner] Lerchner: The Abstraction Fallacy (2026)
- | by: Alexander Lerchner
  A paper arguing against computational functionalism about phenomenal consciousness. CIMC published a reply, 'Against intrinsic properties'.
