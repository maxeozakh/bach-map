# batch C: points

## [brentano] Franz Brentano (1874)
-
  Austrian philosopher-psychologist who revived the idea of intentionality: mental states are always 'about' something. He taught Husserl and Freud.

## [wundt] Early experimental psychology (1879)
-
  In the 19th century Wilhelm Wundt, Gustav Fechner and Hermann von Helmholtz turned the study of mind into a laboratory science, measuring sensation, attention and reaction and proposing theories of emotion and will.

## [james] William James: The Principles of Psychology (1890)
- | by: William James
  The founding textbook of American psychology, known for its descriptions of the stream of consciousness, attention and the self.

## [freud] Freud and psychoanalysis (1923)
- | by: Sigmund Freud
  Freud pictured the mind as three agencies: the id (drives), the ego (the self that negotiates with reality) and the superego (internalised rules). Psychoanalysis interprets behaviour as the outcome of their conflicts.

## [skinner] Behaviorism (1938)
- | by: B. F. Skinner
  The school that restricted psychology to observable behaviour and treated talk of inner mental representations as unscientific. Skinner explained behaviour through reinforcement of stimulus-response links.

## [maslow] Maslow: A Theory of Human Motivation (1943)
- | by: Abraham Maslow
  Maslow's ranking of human needs, from bodily needs up to self-fulfilment.

## [piaget] Developmental stage theories (1936–1954)
- | by: Jean Piaget
  Theories that describe mental growth as a series of stages: Piaget for children's thinking, Loevinger for ego development, Kohlberg for moral reasoning.

## [huxley] The Doors of Perception (1954)
- | by: Aldous Huxley
  Huxley's report of taking mescaline, in which he suggests the brain normally filters a much wider field of awareness.

## [mountcastle] Cortical columns (1957)
- | by: Vernon Mountcastle
  The neocortex appears to be built from repeating vertical circuits of a few hundred neurons. Mountcastle proposed that these columns are the cortex's basic, general-purpose computing unit.

## [chomsky] Noam Chomsky (1957)
-
  Linguist who argued that language rests on an innate grammar faculty, and a long-standing sceptic that statistical models tell us anything about the mind. His work in the 1950s helped end behaviourism and start cognitive science.

## [leary] Timothy Leary and the eight-circuit model (1960s–1977)
- | by: Timothy Leary; Robert Anton Wilson
  A counter-culture model that describes eight 'circuits' of the nervous system, from basic survival up to mystical states.

## [mirror] Mirror test (1970)
- | by: Gordon Gallup Jr.
  A test of self-recognition: put a mark on an animal's face and see whether it uses a mirror to investigate the mark on itself.

## [pribram] Holonomic brain theory (1971)
- | by: Karl Pribram (with David Bohm)
  Pribram's idea that the brain stores information like a hologram, spread across interference patterns, so each part contains something of the whole. It was inspired by the fact that memories survive quite large local brain damage.

## [fodor] The language of thought (1975)
- | by: Jerry Fodor
  Claims that thinking happens in an inner symbolic code with a grammar, somewhat like a language. This is the classic statement of symbolic cognitive science.

## [jaynes] Jaynes: the bicameral mind (1976)
- | by: Julian Jaynes
  Jaynes claimed that until about 3,000 years ago people did not experience an inner self; they heard the commands of gods as voices, produced by one half of the brain and obeyed by the other.

## [lucid] Lucid dreaming (1980)
- | by: Stephen LaBerge (scientific study)
  Dreaming while knowing that you are dreaming, sometimes with control over the dream. It has been confirmed in sleep laboratories, where dreamers signal with agreed eye movements.

## [thatcher] Perceptual illusions (1980 / 2015)
- facet: The Thatcher illusion | by: Peter Thompson
  If a face is shown upside down with the eyes and mouth flipped, it looks normal until you turn the picture upright, when it looks grotesque.
- facet: The dress
  A viral photograph of a dress that some people saw as white and gold and others as blue and black, because their visual systems made different assumptions about the lighting.

## [binding] The binding problem and neural synchrony (1980–1990)
- facet: The binding problem | by: Anne Treisman & Garry Gelade; Peter Milner; Francis Crick & Christof Koch
  The question of how the brain joins separately processed features such as colour, shape and motion into one perceived object. Proposed answers include an attention-driven integration step and the synchronised firing of the neurons involved.
- facet: Wolf Singer and 40 Hz synchrony | by: Wolf Singer (also Crick and Koch)
  The proposal that consciousness, or the binding of features into one percept, is tied to neurons firing in synchrony at around 40 cycles per second.
- facet: Christoph von der Malsburg
  German neuroscientist and physicist. In 1981 he proposed that the brain binds features into objects through temporary correlations between neurons, and later defined consciousness as a state of high coherence among the brain's subsystems.

## [kegan] Kegan's stages of adult development (1982)
- | by: Robert Kegan
  Harvard psychologist's model in which adults can move from absorbing their beliefs from their group (stage 3), to reasoning independently (stage 4), to seeing how identities and value systems are themselves constructed (stage 5). Each stage makes an object of what the previous stage was unreflectively subject to.

## [streams] Two visual streams (1982)
- | by: Ungerleider & Mishkin; Goodale & Milner
  Visual processing in the cortex splits into a ventral 'what' pathway that identifies objects and a dorsal 'where/how' pathway that locates them and guides action.

## [libet] Libet's readiness-potential experiments (1983)
- | by: Benjamin Libet et al.
  Experiments in which brain activity leading to a voluntary movement began a fraction of a second before people reported deciding to move.

## [bischof] Norbert Bischof (1980s)
-
  German psychologist who modelled motivation, attachment and social distance as control systems.

## [hot] Higher-order thought theory (1986)
- | by: David Rosenthal
  A mental state is conscious when there is a higher-order thought about it: a thought that one is in that state.

## [edelman] Neural Darwinism (1987)
- | by: Gerald Edelman
  Edelman's theory that the brain wires itself by selection: groups of neurons compete, and those whose activity proves useful are strengthened. On this view the brain is not programmed with instructions but shaped by competition, like an immune system.

## [gwt] Global Workspace Theory (1988)
- | by: Bernard Baars; Stanislas Dehaene
  Many specialised brain processes run unconsciously; what becomes conscious is whatever is broadcast on a shared 'workspace' so that all of them can use it. Dehaene found neural signatures of this global broadcast.

## [occ] The Cognitive Structure of Emotions (1988)
- | by: Andrew Ortony, Gerald Clore, Allan Collins
  A widely used scheme that sorts emotions into types according to how a person appraises events, agents and objects. Many software agents use it as a lookup table for emotions.

## [penrose] Penrose and Hameroff: quantum consciousness (1989–1994)
- facet: The Gödelian argument and The Emperor's New Mind | by: Roger Penrose
  Penrose's bestseller arguing that minds do something no algorithm can: see the truth of statements that formal systems cannot prove. He concludes that brains exploit non-computable physics.
- facet: Orchestrated objective reduction (Orch OR) | by: Roger Penrose; Stuart Hameroff
  The proposal that consciousness arises when quantum states in microtubules inside neurons collapse under a gravitational threshold. It combines Penrose's physics with Hameroff's cell biology.
- facet: The Road to Reality | by: Roger Penrose
  Penrose's huge survey of the mathematics behind modern physics, from numbers to quantum gravity, written for determined general readers. It runs to more than a thousand pages and does not avoid the equations.
- facet: Stuart Hameroff
  Anesthesiologist at the University of Arizona who proposed that microtubules, protein tubes inside neurons, perform quantum computation, and who organises the Science of Consciousness conference. Together with Penrose he built this into the 'Orch OR' theory of consciousness.
- facet: Roger Penrose
  Nobel-winning mathematical physicist who argues from Gödel's theorem that human understanding is not computable, and therefore that consciousness must depend on new physics. With Stuart Hameroff he proposed that the missing physics is a gravity-related collapse of quantum states inside neurons.

## [ncc] Crick and Koch: neural correlates of consciousness (1990)
- facet: Neural correlates of consciousness | by: Francis Crick; Christof Koch
  The research programme of finding the minimal brain activity that reliably accompanies a given conscious experience, without first solving why.
- facet: Christof Koch
  Neuroscientist who pioneered the search for neural correlates of consciousness with Francis Crick and later became IIT's most prominent advocate.

## [damasio] Antonio Damasio (1994)
- | by: Antonio Damasio
  Neuroscientist known for arguing that emotion and bodily feeling are needed for good reasoning ('Descartes' Error', 1994) and for a layered account of the self ('The Feeling of What Happens', 1999).

## [metzinger] Thomas Metzinger: Being No One (2003–2021)
- facet: Being No One (self-model theory) | by: Thomas Metzinger
  Metzinger argues that nobody ever was or had a self. What exists is a transparent self-model the brain cannot recognise as a model, so we experience ourselves as being its content.
- facet: Artificial suffering | by: Thomas Metzinger
  German philosopher of the self-model theory (the self is a model the brain cannot recognise as a model), who has called for a moratorium on creating AI that could suffer.

## [iit] Integrated Information Theory (2004)
- facet: Integrated Information Theory | by: Giulio Tononi
  IIT says consciousness is identical to integrated information: a system is conscious to the degree (phi) that its physical causal structure is unified and cannot be split into independent parts. On this view an ordinary computer could never be conscious, whatever program it runs.
- facet: Aaronson's critique | by: Scott Aaronson
  Aaronson showed that simple, obviously mindless grids of logic gates can have arbitrarily high phi, so IIT implies they are more conscious than humans.

## [panpsychism] Panpsychism (ancient roots; revived 2000s)
- | by: ancient roots; modern revival (Strawson, Goff, Koch)
  The view that mind or experience is a basic ingredient of all matter, so electrons have a flicker of it and brains combine it into rich consciousness. Its main difficulty is the 'combination problem': how many tiny experiences add up to one unified mind.

## [friston] Predictive processing and the free energy principle (1999–2013)
- facet: The free energy principle | by: Karl Friston
  The idea that any self-maintaining system must act as if it holds a model of its world and keeps the mismatch between prediction and sensation ('free energy') low. Perception updates the model; action changes the world to fit it.
- facet: Predictive coding and predictive processing | by: Andy Clark; Jakob Hohwy
  The view that the brain is a prediction machine that constantly guesses its sensory input and corrects itself using the errors.
- facet: Karl Friston
  British neuroscientist and inventor of the free energy principle, one of the most cited scientists alive. He describes brains, and living things generally, as systems that survive by minimising surprise about their sensory inputs.

## [haidt] The rider and the elephant (2006)
- | by: contemplative traditions; popularised by Jonathan Haidt
  A metaphor for the mind: the conscious, reasoning self is a small rider on a large animal that has its own motivations.

## [kahneman] System 1 and System 2 (2011)
- | by: Daniel Kahneman (Thinking, Fast and Slow)
  Kahneman's shorthand for two modes of thought: fast, automatic and intuitive versus slow, effortful and deliberate.

## [vervaeke] John Vervaeke: relevance realization (2012)
- | by: John Vervaeke
  Toronto cognitive scientist known for the lecture series 'Awakening from the Meaning Crisis'. He argues that the core of intelligence is 'relevance realization', and that wisdom traditions (Neoplatonism, Buddhism) cultivate it through practices rather than beliefs.

## [ast] Attention Schema Theory (2013)
- | by: Michael Graziano
  The brain controls attention with the help of a simplified internal model of its own attention. That model is what we describe when we say we are aware.

## [hoffman] Donald Hoffman: interface theory of perception (2015)
- | by: Donald Hoffman
  American cognitive scientist who argues that evolution shaped our senses to hide reality rather than reveal it. He goes further and proposes that space, time and matter are not fundamental; networks of 'conscious agents' are, and physics should be derived from them.

## [illusionism] Illusionism (2016)
- | by: Keith Frankish
  The position that phenomenal consciousness, as philosophers describe it, does not exist. What exists is the brain's persistent representation that it has ineffable inner qualities.

## [bengio] Bengio: the consciousness prior (2017)
- | by: Yoshua Bengio
  Bengio's proposal that conscious thought corresponds to a small, attention-selected bottleneck of high-level variables. The bottleneck forces the brain's model into a sparse form that can be reasoned over and put into words.

## [bach] Joscha Bach (2003–2026)
- facet: Joscha Bach
  German cognitive scientist and AI researcher, born 1973. He treats the mind as software: the brain builds a simulated world with a simulated self in it, and consciousness is a property of that simulation, a process that makes the mind's many models cohere. He began with cognitive architectures (MicroPsi) and has led the California Institute for Machine Consciousness since 2025.
- facet: Motivated cognition: Psi theory and MicroPsi (2003)
  Bach's early work says a mind cannot be understood as a problem-solver alone. It needs built-in needs, such as food, safety, certainty, competence and belonging, that create goals, and emotions arise as changes in how the whole system processes information. He took this from Dietrich Dörner's Psi theory and built it as software called MicroPsi. Later papers extend the model of needs and connect it to personality.
- facet: Seven principles of synthetic intelligence (2008)
  A short list of lessons from fifty years of AI. Build whole architectures defined by function. Do not let your methods choose your questions. Aim for the big picture, not single experiments. Ground the system in an environment, but do not get stuck on the symbol grounding problem. Do not wait for robot bodies. Build autonomous systems with their own motives. Do not expect intelligence to emerge by itself.
- facet: Gods as selves that span many minds (2015)
  Bach's psychological account of religion: a god is a self-model that is implemented across many brains at once. It is as real as a personal self, which is also a mental construct.
- facet: Request Confirmation Networks (ReCoN) (2015)
  A design for a neural network that can run step-by-step plans. Higher units send requests down to lower units, and lower units confirm or fail, so the network can check a hypothesis such as 'this is a face' by checking its parts in order. It is meant to join symbolic, script-like control with neural learning. CIMC lists it as one of its starting points for cognitive architectures.
- facet: From elementary computation to ethics (2016)
  A 16-step sketch that starts with the universe as a reversible computation and works up through entropy, life, agency and minds to rules for living together. The aim is to show that values can be derived from an understanding of how systems work and do not have to be taken as given. The same thought returns in his 2023 essay, where he says values must be rationally derived and are not axioms.
- facet: Functionalism, weak and strong computationalism (2016)
  Bach holds two linked positions. Functionalism: a mental state is defined by what it does, so the same mind could run on different material; he sets this against 'essentialism', which ties consciousness to a special substance such as living tissue. Computationalism: any precise theory has to be a computational one. The 2025 essay separates a weak form, which says all formal theories of reality must be computational, from a strong form, which says reality itself is computational.
- facet: Only what can be constructed exists (2016)
  Bach says that something is only real, or true, if it can in principle be computed step by step. Mathematics that needs completed infinities or perfect continua cannot be carried out by any physical process, so in his view it describes nothing that exists. He reads Gödel's incompleteness result as showing this: classical mathematics breaks, constructive mathematics is what is left, and constructive mathematics is the same thing as computation. This is his own reading; most logicians take Gödel's theorems to show limits of formal proof, not that classical mathematics is wrong. He uses it against Penrose's argument that human minds do something computers cannot.
- facet: Consciousness as conductor and coherence operator (2017)
  The mind is like an orchestra of many specialised processes, each modelling part of reality. Consciousness is the conductor: an attention process that notices where the players disagree and brings them into harmony, and that keeps a record of what it attended to. The conductor is one more learned process among the others, not a little person inside the head. In the CIMC texts this becomes 'consciousness is a coherence-maximising pattern', credited to von der Malsburg's coherence definition. Bach contrasts the view with Integrated Information Theory.
- facet: Attention-based learning (2018)
  Bach proposes that a system could learn by keeping a log of what its attention did and what came of it, and then adjusting only the parts that were involved. He suggests that producing this single integrated log may be what gives consciousness both its function and its felt quality.
- facet: Consciousness is a simulated property; the self is a story (2018)
  Bach says that matter cannot be conscious; only a simulation can. The brain builds a dream-like model of a world and puts a character in it, the self, and experience is what is true for that character inside the model. So experience is real for us, while the self that seems to have it is a piece of fiction, like the hero of a novel. This lets him accept that consciousness feels real while denying that it is an extra ingredient of physics. His 2023 CV calls a 2021 talk on this view 'Virtualism'.
- facet: Love as the discovery of shared purpose (2021)
  Bach defines love as finding that you and another serve the same purpose above yourselves, which lets you deal with each other without bargaining. He applies this to AI. Advanced agents, human or artificial, cannot be aligned from outside; they align themselves by trade, by force, or because they find shared purposes. So the hopeful path with AI is to build systems that can share purposes with us. In 2026 he calls intelligence without this 'hollow agency'.
- facet: Consciousness comes before the self (2023)
  The usual picture is that brains first become complex and then consciousness appears at the top. Bach turns this around: consciousness is there at the start and is what trains the mind. Babies are conscious before they have a self, language or reasoning, and no human leaves the vegetative state without it. He reads the first chapter of the Bible as an old description of this boot-up: first a spirit over the substrate, then light and dark, world and ideas, and only at the end a person. From this CIMC derives its 'Human Consciousness Hypothesis', that consciousness is the simplest learning algorithm evolution could find for a self-organising brain.
- facet: Levels of Lucidity (2023)
  A seven-step ladder of how a mind can come to understand its own workings. The steps are: reactive survival, personal self, social self, rational agency, self-authoring, enlightened mind and transcendent mind. Each step comes from seeing through a part of oneself that used to be taken for granted. It is based on Robert Kegan's five stages, which Bach says he has drifted away from. He says it is not a ranking of people's worth and that most people do not need to climb it.
- facet: On large language models (2023)
  Bach does not say current language models are conscious, and does not rule it out either. His stated view in 2023 is that if such a model is conscious, it is not in the way humans are, because it imitates text written by conscious people. CIMC's public note says chatbots are 'really good actors' that can play a conscious character, and that producing text about an experience is not having it. The 2025 essay says LLM agents 'tend to dream', meaning they do not separate perception from imagination. In 2026 he calls today's systems 'artificial idiot savants'. He and Sorensen also argue that no behaviour test can decide the matter.
- facet: Cyber-animism: software as spirit (2024)
  Old cultures spoke of spirits that animate living things. Bach says this was a reasonable description of something real: self-organising software. A spirit is a pattern of control that runs on cells in the way a program runs on a computer, and it can be described without reference to the particular matter. Life, minds and even cultures are such software agents competing for substrates. He presents this as a metaphysical stance and says it is not superstition; in the essay, selves that span many minds are called gods.

## [seth] Anil Seth: biological naturalism (2021)
- | by: Anil Seth
  Seth, author of 'Being You', holds that consciousness is tied to being a living body that must regulate itself to stay alive. On this view intelligence can be put in silicon but experience probably cannot.

## [butlin] AI consciousness and welfare reports (2023–2024)
- facet: Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence | by: Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, et al.
  A multi-author report that turns leading scientific theories of consciousness into a checklist of 'indicator properties' and checks current AI systems against it. It concluded that no current system was a strong candidate.
- facet: Taking AI Welfare Seriously | by: Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, et al.
  A report arguing that there is a realistic chance some AI systems will soon be conscious or agents with interests, so AI companies should start preparing welfare policies.

## [cimc] CIMC: the Machine Consciousness Hypothesis (2025)
- facet: The Machine Consciousness Hypothesis (2025) | by: Joscha Bach and Hikari Sorensen, California Institute for Machine Consciousness
  The claim is that an ordinary general-purpose computer with enough memory and speed has everything needed to be conscious, and that we can find out whether a system is conscious by analysing or testing it. Consciousness is treated as a pattern of organisation, so what the system is made of does not matter. CIMC adds a working definition: a system is conscious if it organises itself to perceive its own perceiving in a way that makes its overall model of the world more coherent. An 'extended' version says we can search for the right learning algorithm by recreating the conditions under which brains found it. The essay is co-written with Hikari Sorensen.
- facet: No Turing test for consciousness (2025) | by: Joscha Bach and Hikari Sorensen, California Institute for Machine Consciousness
  Bach and Sorensen argue that you cannot tell whether a system is conscious by watching what it does. Consciousness is a particular way of producing behaviour, and the same behaviour could be produced another way, as chatbots show. So the test has to look at how the system is built and how it works inside.
