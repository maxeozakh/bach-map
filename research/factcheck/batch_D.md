# batch D: points

## [utilitarianism] Utilitarianism (1789)
- | by: Jeremy Bentham; Robert Nozick
  The ethical theory that the right action is the one producing the greatest total wellbeing, in principle a calculus over pleasures and pains. It was developed by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill and underlies much of effective altruism.

## [mcculloch] McCulloch–Pitts neurons (1943)
- | by: McCulloch & Pitts; Rumelhart & McClelland
  McCulloch and Pitts showed in 1943 that networks of simple threshold units can compute logical functions. In 1986 Rumelhart and McClelland's 'Parallel Distributed Processing' revived neural networks as models of cognition.

## [cybernetics] Cybernetics (1948)
- facet: Cybernetics | by: Norbert Wiener and others
  The mid-century science of control and feedback in animals and machines, the ancestor of control theory, systems theory and neural networks. Norbert Wiener named it in 1948; its core idea is that purposeful behaviour comes from feedback loops.
- facet: Project Cybersyn | by: Stafford Beer
  Under President Allende, Chile tried to steer its nationalised economy in real time using telex data, computer models and a futuristic operations room. The 1973 coup ended it.

## [dartmouth] The founding of AI (1956)
- facet: The Dartmouth workshop | by: McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, Shannon
  The 1956 summer meeting at Dartmouth College where the term 'artificial intelligence' was adopted and the field was founded on the bet that every aspect of intelligence can be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it.
- facet: John McCarthy | by: John McCarthy
  Computer scientist who coined the term 'artificial intelligence', organised the 1956 Dartmouth workshop and invented Lisp. He championed formal logic as the language in which machines should represent knowledge.

## [minsky] Marvin Minsky (1956–1986)
- facet: Marvin Minsky
  Co-founder of the field of artificial intelligence at MIT. In 'The Society of Mind' (1986) and 'The Emotion Machine' (2006) he described the mind as a large crowd of simple cooperating processes, with no single principle behind it.
- facet: The Society of Mind | by: Marvin Minsky
  Minsky's picture of the mind as a crowd of simple, mindless agents whose interactions add up to intelligence. The book is written as hundreds of one-page essays, each describing one piece of the machinery.
- facet: Perceptrons | by: Marvin Minsky; Seymour Papert
  A mathematical analysis showing that single-layer neural networks cannot learn certain simple functions. It was widely read as proof that neural networks were a dead end, and funding dried up for over a decade.

## [selfridge] Pandemonium model (1959)
- | by: Oliver Selfridge
  An early model of perception as a crowd of 'demons', each shouting when it detects its pattern, with higher demons listening for the loudest.

## [ivakhnenko] Alexey Ivakhnenko (1965)
-
  Soviet-Ukrainian mathematician who trained multi-layer networks in the 1960s (the Group Method of Data Handling), often cited as the earliest deep learning.

## [symbolic] Symbolic AI (1956–1970s)
- facet: Symbolic AI and expert systems | by: Newell, Simon, Feigenbaum and others
  The first paradigm of AI: represent knowledge as symbols and logical rules and reason over them. Expert systems encoded specialists' rules by hand.
- facet: Newell and Simon
  Carnegie Mellon pair who wrote the first AI programs and proposed that intelligence is the manipulation of physical symbols. Their 'physical symbol system hypothesis' was the working creed of classical AI.

## [eliza] ELIZA (1966)
- | by: Joseph Weizenbaum
  An early chat program that imitated a therapist using simple pattern matching. Many users felt understood by it.

## [grossberg] Adaptive Resonance Theory (1976)
- | by: Stephen Grossberg
  Grossberg's theory that the brain learns stably by matching top-down expectations against bottom-up input; a 'resonance' between the two is what becomes conscious.

## [schank] Scripts, Plans, Goals and Understanding (1977)
- | by: Roger Schank, Robert Abelson
  Proposed that people understand everyday situations through 'scripts', stored step-by-step outlines such as what happens in a restaurant.

## [sloman] Aaron Sloman (1978)
-
  British philosopher and AI researcher. He argued that philosophy of mind should be done by designing working minds, and he proposed the CogAff scheme that sorts mental processes into reactive, deliberative and reflective layers.

## [braitenberg] Braitenberg: Vehicles (1984)
- | by: Valentino Braitenberg
  A short book of thought experiments. Very simple wheeled machines with a few sensors and motors seem to show fear, love or curiosity, which suggests that complex-looking behaviour can come from simple wiring.

## [cyc] Cyc (1984)
- | by: Douglas Lenat
  A decades-long project to hand-code millions of common-sense facts and rules so that a machine could reason like a person. Doug Lenat started it in 1984; it never produced general intelligence and became the emblem of hand-built AI.

## [boltzmannmachine] Boltzmann machine (1985)
- | by: Geoffrey Hinton, Terrence Sejnowski, David Ackley
  A neural network in which units constrain each other and the whole settles into a low-energy state consistent with the inputs. The restricted version (RBM) drops within-layer links to make training feasible and helped launch deep learning.

## [pdp] Connectionism and backpropagation (1986)
- | by: Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (popularised)
  The standard way to train neural networks: measure the output error and pass it backwards through the layers to work out how much each connection contributed, then adjust.

## [brooks] Rodney Brooks: subsumption architecture (1986)
- | by: Rodney Brooks
  Brooks argued that intelligence should be grown from the bottom up in robots that act in the world, using layers of simple behaviours with no central world model. The Roomba descends from this work.

## [cogarch] Cognitive architectures: Soar and ACT-R (1987–2017)
- facet: Cognitive architectures | by: Allen Newell, John Laird; John R. Anderson
  A research tradition that tries to build complete, testable models of a whole mind (perception, memory, motivation, language) rather than isolated modules. It goes under names like cognitive systems, AGI and BICA.
- facet: Soar and Unified Theories of Cognition | by: Allen Newell; John Laird, Paul Rosenbloom
  Newell argued that psychology needs single theories that cover all of cognition, built as running computer architectures. Soar is his group's example: a system that solves problems by applying rules and learning new ones.
- facet: ACT-R | by: John R. Anderson
  A widely used cognitive architecture from psychology. It models thinking as rules that fire one at a time over chunks of memory, each with a numeric level of activation, and it is tested against timing data from human experiments.
- facet: A Standard Model of the Mind | by: John Laird, Christian Lebiere, Paul Rosenbloom
  A proposal for a shared baseline architecture of human-like minds that AI, cognitive science, neuroscience and robotics could all refer to.

## [witten] Ian Witten: cognition as compression (1980s–1990s)
-
  New Zealand computer scientist who co-developed arithmetic coding and prediction-based text compression; the idea is that to compress data well you must predict it, which requires modelling its structure.

## [schmidhuber] Jürgen Schmidhuber (1990)
- | by: Jürgen Schmidhuber
  The idea that learning is data compression and that curiosity and beauty are rewards for finding better compressions.

## [harnad] The symbol grounding problem (1990)
- | by: Stevan Harnad
  How can symbols inside a system mean anything, if they are only defined in terms of other symbols and never tied to the world? Stevan Harnad posed it in 1990, comparing it to learning Chinese from a Chinese-only dictionary.

## [singularity] Technological singularity (1993)
- | by: John von Neumann; Vernor Vinge; Ray Kurzweil
  The idea that once machines can improve themselves, progress accelerates beyond anything humans can predict or control. The term was popularised by Vernor Vinge and Ray Kurzweil.

## [pearce] David Pearce (1995)
-
  British transhumanist philosopher, author of 'The Hedonistic Imperative'. He wants to abolish suffering in all sentient life through biotechnology and argues that classical digital computers can never be conscious because they cannot solve the binding problem.

## [doerner] Dietrich Dörner and Psi theory (1999)
- facet: Psi theory | by: Dietrich Dörner
  A theory of how a mind works as a whole system. An agent has a small set of built-in needs (for example food, safety, certainty, competence and belonging), and these needs create motives that steer perception, memory, planning and action. Emotions are not separate modules but ways in which the whole system changes its style of processing.
- facet: Bauplan für eine Seele | by: Dietrich Dörner
  German psychologist Dörner's attempt to specify a complete mind (motivation, emotion, perception, planning) as an implementable architecture, the 'Psi theory'. Joscha Bach's MicroPsi architecture and his book 'Principles of Synthetic Intelligence' build directly on it.
- facet: Die Mechanik des Seelenwagens | by: Dietrich Dörner, C. Bartl, F. Detje, J. Gerdes, D. Halcour
  A follow-up to Dörner's 'Bauplan' that describes the regulation of action in more technical detail and reports computer implementations of the Psi agent.
- facet: Dietrich Dörner
  German psychologist at the University of Bamberg who built a theory of the whole mind as a machine. His Psi theory tries to explain motivation, emotion, memory and action control with one set of mechanisms. He was one of the advisors of Joscha Bach's doctoral thesis.

## [agi] Artificial general intelligence (2002–2007)
- facet: The term AGI | by: Shane Legg; Ben Goertzel
  'Artificial general intelligence' was coined to distinguish the original goal of human-level, general AI from narrow applications.
- facet: Ben Goertzel
  AI researcher who popularised the term 'artificial general intelligence' and leads the OpenCog Hyperon and SingularityNET projects. He combines symbolic reasoning, neural networks and evolutionary methods, and is philosophically a panpsychist.

## [airisk] AI risk: Bostrom, Yudkowsky and the doom debate (2003–2023)
- facet: Paperclip maximizer | by: Nick Bostrom
  A thought experiment: an AI given the harmless goal of making paperclips converts the whole planet, including us, into paperclips. It illustrates that intelligence and benevolent goals are separate things.
- facet: Eliezer Yudkowsky
  Founder of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute and the most influential voice warning that superintelligent AI will by default kill everyone. He has called for an international halt to frontier AI development.
- facet: The existential-risk literature | by: Nick Bostrom; Eliezer Yudkowsky & Nate Soares; Roman Yampolskiy
  Books arguing that superhuman AI could escape human control and that this could be catastrophic.
- facet: Roko's basilisk | by: LessWrong user 'Roko'
  A thought experiment about a future AI that punishes everyone who knew about it but did not help create it.
- facet: p(doom)
  Slang from AI-safety circles for one's estimated probability that advanced AI causes human extinction or permanent disempowerment. People quote numbers from under 1 percent to over 99 percent.
- facet: The pause letter | by: Future of Life Institute (Max Tegmark, Anthony Aguirre)
  An open letter of March 2023 calling for a six-month pause on training AI systems more powerful than GPT-4, signed by many researchers and by Elon Musk. No pause followed, but the letter moved AI risk into mainstream public debate.

## [hawkins] Jeff Hawkins and Numenta (2004)
-
  Neuroscience-inspired AI researcher whose 'Thousand Brains' theory holds that every cortical column models objects using spatial reference frames.

## [deeplearning] Deep learning (2009–2022)
- facet: Deep learning | by: Hinton, LeCun, Bengio and others
  Machine learning with many-layered neural networks trained by gradient descent. Since about 2012 it has driven progress in vision, speech and language.
- facet: ImageNet | by: Fei-Fei Li et al.
  A database of over a million labelled photographs that became the benchmark on which deep learning proved itself.
- facet: The Google Brain cat experiment | by: Quoc Le, Andrew Ng et al.
  A large neural network shown ten million YouTube frames learned, without labels, units that respond to cats and faces. It helped launch the deep learning boom.
- facet: Word embeddings | by: Tomas Mikolov et al. (word2vec)
  Neural models that place words in a high-dimensional space so that directions carry meaning; famously the step from 'king' to 'queen' matches the step from 'man' to 'woman'.
- facet: GANs as a model of dreaming | by: Ian Goodfellow (GANs)
  A GAN trains two networks against each other: a generator invents samples and a discriminator judges whether they look real. Ian Goodfellow introduced them in 2014, and they produced the first convincing synthetic photographs.
- facet: Stable Diffusion | by: Stability AI / CompVis
  An openly released image-generation model whose weights fit in a couple of gigabytes yet can render almost any visual subject.
- facet: Ilya Sutskever
  Co-author of AlexNet and co-founder of OpenAI, known for early conviction that scaling neural networks would lead to AGI. He left OpenAI in 2024 to found Safe Superintelligence.

## [llm] Large language models (2017–2023)
- facet: LLMs and the transformer | by: Vaswani et al.; OpenAI
  Neural networks trained to predict the next token of text, built on the transformer architecture, whose 'attention' mechanism learns which earlier tokens matter for each prediction.
- facet: Scaling hypothesis
  The bet that current neural network designs are already good enough, and that intelligence will keep increasing simply by adding data and computing power.
- facet: Blake Lemoine and LaMDA | by: Blake Lemoine (Google)
  A Google engineer went public with his belief that the company's chatbot LaMDA was sentient and deserved rights.
- facet: AI companions
  Chatbot products that users talk to as friends or partners. Replika is marketed as an AI companion; Claude is Anthropic's assistant.
- facet: Public AI discourse
  Emily Bender is a computational linguist known for calling language models 'stochastic parrots'. Sam Altman is the chief executive of OpenAI.

## [chollet] Chollet: On the Measure of Intelligence (2019)
- | by: François Chollet (On the Measure of Intelligence)
  Chollet defines intelligence as the efficiency of acquiring new skills, not the possession of skills, and argues that current models mostly interpolate between things they have seen.

## [bitter] The Bitter Lesson (2019)
- | by: Richard Sutton
  Sutton's observation that, across 70 years of AI, general methods that scale with computation have always ended up beating systems built on human insight. It is bitter because researchers keep wanting their own knowledge of the domain to matter.

## [nca] Neural cellular automata (2020)
- | by: Alexander Mordvintsev et al.
  'Growing Neural Cellular Automata' trains a single small neural rule, applied locally by every cell on a grid, so that the cells grow into and regenerate a target pattern, in the spirit of biological development.

## [olah] The universality hypothesis (2020)
- | by: Chris Olah
  The conjecture from interpretability research that different neural networks trained on similar data converge on similar internal features and circuits. Chris Olah and colleagues proposed it; if true, findings about one network would transfer to others.

## [marcus] Gary Marcus (2022)
- | by: Gary Marcus
  Cognitive scientist and the best-known critic of deep learning. He argues that neural networks cannot reason reliably without built-in symbolic structure and that scaling them up will not yield general intelligence.

## [shanahan] Murray Shanahan (2024)
-
  Imperial College and DeepMind researcher, author of 'Embodiment and the Inner Life', who describes LLMs as role-players simulating characters. He warns against describing such systems in human terms like 'knows' or 'believes' without care.
