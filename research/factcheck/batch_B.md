# batch B: points

## [pythagoras] Pythagoras (6th c. BCE)
-
  Greek thinker whose school held that number is the basis of reality. The school was famously disturbed by the discovery of irrational quantities.

## [celltheory] Cell theory (1839)
- | by: Matthias Schleiden; Theodor Schwann
  The discovery that all living things are made of cells and that the cell is the basic unit of life.

## [darwin] Charles Darwin (1859)
- | by: Charles Darwin (remark attributed to Mikhail Gromov)
  Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection explained the design of living things without a designer.

## [cantor] Cantor's set theory (1874)
- | by: Georg Cantor
  Cantor showed that infinite sets come in different sizes: the set of all subsets of a set is always larger than the set itself. Applied to 'the set of all sets' this produces contradictions.

## [frege] Gottlob Frege (1879)
- | by: Gottlob Frege; Alfred Tarski
  German logician who invented modern predicate logic and tried to derive arithmetic from pure logic. Russell found a paradox in his system, which set off the crisis in the foundations of mathematics.

## [peano] Peano axioms (1889)
- | by: Giuseppe Peano
  A handful of axioms that define the natural numbers and arithmetic: there is a zero, every number has a successor, and induction holds.

## [brouwer] Constructive mathematics and intuitionism (1908)
- | by: L. E. J. Brouwer (intuitionism)
  A school of mathematics that accepts an object only if you can give a procedure to construct it, rejecting proofs that merely assert existence and treating infinity as a never-finished process.

## [russell] Russell and Whitehead (1901–1913)
- facet: Russell's paradox | by: Bertrand Russell
  Consider the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Does it contain itself? If it does, it doesn't; if it doesn't, it does. The paradox showed that naive set theory is inconsistent.
- facet: Principia Mathematica | by: Bertrand Russell, Alfred North Whitehead
  A three-volume attempt to derive all of mathematics from logic, using a hierarchy of 'types' to block self-referential paradoxes such as the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Gödel's 1931 incompleteness theorems were proved about this very system.

## [hilbert] Hilbert's programme (1920s)
- | by: David Hilbert
  Hilbert asked mathematicians to prove, by purely mechanical means, that mathematics is consistent and complete, so that every true statement could in principle be derived.

## [goedel] Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931)
- | by: Kurt Gödel
  Gödel proved that any consistent formal system rich enough to do arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and cannot prove its own consistency. He did it by encoding statements about the system as numbers inside the system.

## [tarski] Alfred Tarski (1933)
-
  Polish logician who gave the standard formal definition of truth for logical languages and put semantics on a rigorous footing.

## [turing] Alan Turing and computability (1936–1950)
- facet: Alan Turing
  English mathematician who defined computation with his abstract machine, helped found AI with the 1950 imitation-game paper, and late in life modelled how chemical patterns form in biology. He also led the breaking of the German Enigma cipher during the Second World War.
- facet: Church–Turing thesis | by: Alonzo Church; Alan Turing
  Everything that can be effectively computed can be computed by a Turing machine, so all sufficiently powerful computers are equivalent in what they can compute. It is a thesis rather than a theorem, because 'effectively computable' is an informal notion.
- facet: The Turing machine | by: Alan Turing
  An imaginary device with a tape, a read-write head and a table of rules. Despite its simplicity it can carry out any calculation that any computer can, which makes it the standard definition of computation.
- facet: Halting problem | by: Alan Turing
  Turing showed that no general procedure can decide, for every program, whether it will eventually stop or run forever. Some well-posed questions therefore have no computable answer.
- facet: Post machine | by: Emil Post
  A model of computation proposed by Emil Post in the same year as Turing's, built from very simple instructions on a tape of marked and unmarked boxes.
- facet: Computing Machinery and Intelligence (the Turing test) | by: Alan Turing
  The paper that asks 'Can machines think?' and replaces the question with an imitation game, later called the Turing test: if a machine can hold a conversation indistinguishable from a human's, we should grant it intelligence. It also answers a list of objections, including one from telepathy.

## [lambda] Lambda calculus and combinators (1924–1936)
- facet: Lambda calculus | by: Alonzo Church
  Different formal definitions of what it means to compute (Turing's machines, Church's lambda calculus, Schönfinkel's combinators) turned out to be equivalent. The Church-Turing thesis says they capture everything that can be computed at all.
- facet: Moses Schönfinkel
  Logician who invented combinatory logic, showing that all functions can be built from a couple of basic combinators, a precursor of the lambda calculus and functional programming.

## [schroedinger] Schrödinger: life as negentropy (1944)
- | by: Erwin Schrödinger
  In 'What is Life?' Schrödinger suggested that organisms stay ordered by feeding on 'negative entropy': they export disorder to their environment.

## [vonneumann] John von Neumann and the first computers (1837–1956)
- facet: John von Neumann
  Hungarian-American polymath who shaped game theory, quantum mechanics and the stored-program computer architecture that bears his name. He also designed self-reproducing automata, an early formal model of life.
- facet: Babbage, Zuse, von Neumann
  Babbage designed a mechanical general-purpose computer in the 1830s; Zuse built the first working programmable computers in Germany around 1941; von Neumann defined the stored-program architecture still used today.
- facet: Reliable organisms from unreliable components | by: John von Neumann
  Showed how to build reliable computation out of noisy, failure-prone parts by using redundancy.

## [shannon] Claude Shannon and information theory (1948–1950)
- facet: Information theory | by: Claude Shannon
  Shannon defined information as reduction of uncertainty, measurable in bits, and showed how much of it can be sent over a noisy channel.
- facet: Computer chess | by: Claude Shannon
  Shannon's paper 'Programming a Computer for Playing Chess' distinguished between searching all moves to a fixed depth and selectively exploring promising lines.

## [morpho] Turing's morphogenesis (1952)
- | by: Alan Turing
  Turing showed that interacting chemicals that diffuse at different rates spontaneously form spots and stripes. Neural cellular automata are a modern descendant: grids of identical cells that learn local rules to grow and repair a pattern.

## [everett] Many-worlds interpretation (1957)
- | by: Hugh Everett III
  The reading of quantum mechanics in which the wave function never collapses; every possible outcome happens in a separate branch of the universe. Hugh Everett proposed it in 1957; observers split along with everything else, so each copy sees a single outcome.

## [solomonoff] Solomonoff induction (1964)
- | by: Ray Solomonoff
  Inventor of algorithmic probability, a mathematically ideal (though uncomputable) theory of learning by preferring the shortest programs that explain the data. He attended the 1956 Dartmouth workshop and his ideas underlie later formal theories of universal intelligence.

## [digital] Digital physics (1969)
- | by: Konrad Zuse
  The hypothesis that the universe is, at bottom, a discrete computation such as a cellular automaton. Zuse proposed it in 'Rechnender Raum' (1969); Fredkin and Wolfram developed it.

## [conway] Conway's Game of Life (1970)
- | by: John Conway
  A grid of cells that live or die according to how many neighbours they have. From these few rules arise gliders, self-copying patterns and even complete computers.

## [gaia] Gaia hypothesis (1972)
- | by: James Lovelock; Lynn Margulis
  The proposal that life on Earth, taken together, regulates the planet's atmosphere and climate as if it were a single organism. James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis proposed it in the 1970s.

## [groupsel] Multilevel selection (1975)
- | by: D. S. Wilson; E. O. Wilson
  Natural selection acts at several levels at once: selfish individuals beat altruists within a group, but groups of altruists beat groups of egoists.

## [dawkins] Dawkins: selfish genes and memes (1976)
- | by: Richard Dawkins
  Dawkins reframed evolution as competition between genes, with organisms as the vehicles genes build to get themselves copied. The book also introduced the 'meme'.

## [mandelbrot] The Mandelbrot set (1980)
- | by: Benoît Mandelbrot
  The Mandelbrot set is an infinitely detailed shape generated by repeating one tiny formula. It is a standard example of unlimited complexity from a simple rule.

## [quantumcomp] Quantum computing (1982)
- | by: Richard Feynman; David Deutsch
  Computers that use quantum superposition and entanglement to solve certain problems, such as factoring, far faster than any classical machine could. Building them is hard because quantum states are destroyed by the slightest disturbance from the environment.

## [sicp] Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs (1985)
- | by: Harold Abelson; Gerald Jay Sussman
  MIT's classic introductory programming textbook. It opens by describing computational processes as abstract beings that inhabit computers and are conjured by programs, like spirits by spells.

## [autopoiesis] Autocatalytic networks and autopoiesis (1972–1986)
- | by: Stuart Kauffman; Wim Hordijk & Mike Steel; Humberto Maturana & Francisco Varela
  Models of how a set of parts can come to produce and maintain itself as a whole, first used to explain the origin of life. Autopoiesis is the related idea of a system that continually makes its own components.

## [hypercomputation] Hypercomputation (1990)
-
  Hypothetical machines that compute more than a Turing machine, for example by using infinitely precise real numbers ('geometric') or by sending results back in time ('causal').

## [wheeler] It from bit (1990)
- | by: John Archibald Wheeler
  Wheeler's slogan for the idea that information is more basic than matter and energy: every physical 'it' comes from yes-or-no answers, 'bits'.

## [wolfram] Stephen Wolfram (2002–2023)
- facet: Stephen Wolfram
  Physicist and founder of Wolfram Research. 'A New Kind of Science' (2002) argues that simple programs underlie nature; his 2023 essay 'Observer Theory' asks how the kind of observer we are shapes the laws we find.
- facet: The Ruliad | by: Stephen Wolfram
  Wolfram's name for the result of running every possible computational rule in every possible way: a single object containing all formal possibilities. Observers like us sample a thin slice of it, which we call the physical universe.
- facet: Computational irreducibility | by: Stephen Wolfram
  Some processes cannot be shortcut: the only way to learn what they will do is to run them step by step. This limits prediction even in a fully deterministic universe.
- facet: Observer Theory | by: Stephen Wolfram
  An essay asking what it means to be an observer in a computational universe, and how an observer's limits determine the physical laws it perceives.

## [fields] Chris Fields (2012)
-
  Independent physicist who applies quantum information theory to biology and cognition, often with Michael Levin and Karl Friston. He treats any system with a boundary as an observer exchanging information with its environment.

## [deutsch] David Deutsch: constructor theory (2012)
- | by: David Deutsch; Chiara Marletto
  Deutsch proposes restating physics in terms of which transformations are possible and which are impossible, rather than initial conditions and laws of motion. He also holds that science advances by explanation, not prediction.

## [ackley] Best-effort computing (2010s)
- | by: Dave Ackley
  Ackley's research programme on computers that keep working when their parts are unreliable, in the manner of living tissue, instead of assuming perfect hardware.

## [tegmark] Max Tegmark (2014–2023)
- facet: Max Tegmark
  MIT physicist known for the mathematical universe hypothesis and for work on physics-based theories of consciousness.
- facet: Consciousness as a State of Matter | by: Max Tegmark
  A physicist's proposal to treat consciousness as a state of matter with certain information-processing properties, building on IIT.
- facet: Language Models Represent Space and Time | by: Wes Gurnee and Max Tegmark
  A study showing that a large language model's internal activations contain linear representations of where places are on Earth and when events happened, in effect a map. The authors, Wes Gurnee and Max Tegmark, take this as evidence that such models learn a world model and not just word statistics.
- facet: Life 3.0 | by: Max Tegmark
  Tegmark sorts life into three stages: biological (hardware and software evolve), cultural (software is learned), and technological (both can be designed), and discusses the future with AI.

## [aaronson] Scott Aaronson (2014)
-
  Quantum-complexity theorist at UT Austin who spent time at OpenAI working on the theoretical foundations of AI safety. He takes AI risk seriously while rejecting certainty of doom.

## [thooft] 't Hooft: cellular automaton interpretation (2016)
- | by: Gerard 't Hooft
  Nobel laureate 't Hooft argues that quantum mechanics could be a statistical description of an underlying deterministic, discrete system like a cellular automaton.

## [levin] Michael Levin: bioelectricity and collective intelligence (2019)
- | by: Michael Levin
  Tufts biologist who studies how groups of cells use bioelectric signalling to decide what body to build. He treats cells and tissues as agents with goals, so that anatomy is the result of a collective intelligence solving problems.

## [metazoa] Godfrey-Smith: Metazoa (2020)
- | by: Peter Godfrey-Smith
  A philosopher-diver traces how experience may have arisen step by step in the evolution of animal bodies and nervous systems.
