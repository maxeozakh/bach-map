The unexpected revolution
In 1943, Thomas J. Watson, the chairman and president of IBM, famously predicted that the world market might not need more than five computers. In 1977, Ken Olson, founder and CEO of Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC), maintained: “There is no reason for any individual to have a computer in their home.” Six years later, I had convinced my grandparents to buy a home computer for me and taught myself how to program. I quickly realized that this technology was unlike anything else in human history. The computer allows us turn our thoughts into living functions: as long as we are able to express an idea so precisely that we can break it down to first principles, it is only speed and memory of the computer that limit what it lets us create. It seemed obvious to me that we should want to create minds to put into the computer, so it might become itself creative, intelligent, capable of talking to us and allowing us to meet new kinds of beings.
The same idea, teaching computers how to think, had fascinated Alan Turing, John von Neumann, Marvin Minsky, John McCarthy and others when they started the project of Artificial Intelligence in the 1950ies. Our cultural mainstream met AI with skepticism, not so much because people were worried about dangerous consequences, but because the idea of creating artificial minds seemed so outlandish and improbable that reasonable people considered it to be a preposterous fantasy. Even AI researchers doubted the possibility of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI, systems that would operate at the human level and beyond), and for most, AI became synonymous with Narrow AI, that is, with technical systems that automate problems for which humans need intelligence.
Despite the steady and undeniable progress of AI and its applications, widespread AGI skepticism dominated well into the 2010s, when Deep Learning innovations scaled up Rosenblatt’s Perceptron (1957), and the Backpropagation algorithm (discovered multiple times, for instance by Paul Werbos in 1974) to the point where it could process large datasets containing images, sound or text. In the year 2020, the highly publicized feats of a Large Language Model by OpenAI turned GPT-3 (for Generalized Pretrained Transformer Mk 3) into a household name and started the LLM era.
Science fiction shows like “Star Trek” had long familiarized us with computers that could understand naturally spoken language, demonstrate common sense, translate fluently between arbitrary human languages, but before LLMs, these applications seemed to be far in the future. Ironically, it would have been possible to build useful LLMs as early as the 1990ies (albeit at smaller scale and higher cost), had AGI research not languished in relative obscurity due to widespread culturally motivated doubts.
The speed of progress in the last few years leaves us reeling. The prospect of augmenting and replacing knowledge work, driving, education, finance, media production and many other human economic activities with machines is threatening much more disruptive changes than any previous technological revolution, and it appears to be happening on a shorter timescale.
The current generation of AI systems is also a surprise for many researchers who have been working in the field of AI themselves. Quite a few of us expected that Artificial General Intelligence would slowly emerge over a multitude of incrementally discovered, intricately interlocking modules and mechanisms, together reproducing the modularity of the human mind. Others hoped for some kind of master algorithm, geared to discover truth from first principles, surpassing the strength of human logic but — at least initially — staying behind in areas like common sense reasoning, specialized knowledge and comprehension of everyday real-world situations. Instead, we got unexpected capabilities out of systems that imitate human media production as statistical patterns. Our first generation AGIs are artificial idiot savants that know nearly everything that has ever been written, yet are still prone to making stunning logical mistakes.
Surprisingly, this first wave of general AI has also turned out to be more democratic and equitable than most pundits expected, and arguably much more than its gets credit for: despite the astronomic costs that go into training current AI models, there are no monopolies on the technology. Practically everyone can access the same powerful models as large companies for very moderate subscription fees. Industry strength AI models keep getting released into the public domain, so that academics and private individuals can (and do) participate in frontier research. The leading AI companies do their best to make their models as safe, useful and equitable as they can. This does not mean that there is no reason to be concerned about the existing and future effects of this technological revolution, on the stability of societies, the functioning of our economies and the future of humanity.
What will happen if AI systems can perform most of the work that humans can do through a screen, keyboard and speaker, with much greater speed, reliability and no trace of boredom? What if dextrous robots don’t just dominate the factory floor, but also clean, repair, cook or make deliveries? Will AI generated social media and companion agents destroy our mental health and sap our ability to enter meaningful relationships with each other? In a world run by centralized AI, can universal basic income give us freedom for self actualization or will it condemn us to a passive existence under ubiquitous surveillance, ensuring that our growing discontent does not lead to crime and political uprisings? Or can decentralized AI empower all of us, endowing every human with universal basic intelligence, so we can enter meaningful contracts with everyone at the same time, replacing poorly incentivized, inefficient and intransparent governments with an unceasing, truthful and fully coherent negotiation of the interests of every citizen?
The changes that are being wrought by AGI are not only going deeper and are happening faster than most of us could predict, they make it necessary to enter a completely new level of discourse. AGI is not simply going to change how we produce goods and services, how we work and learn, how we create and entertain ourselves. AI will profoundly change our identity. The AGI age forces us to rethink our relationship to ourselves, each other, our future and nature. If you are interested in entering this discourse, then this book is for you.
From Artificial General Intelligence to Artificial General Experience
How is AI going to change humanity? How will it change our relationship to ourselves? How are we going to live, love, and raise children in this new age? The scope of Rana Gujral’s thinking is exceptional. His book will not only give you highly informed insight into the multitude of applications into which AI is currently being deployed (including education, creativity, industry, transportation, military, administration, medicine, law, business and finance), what changes and difficulties to expect, and how to find your way as an active participant into these developments. Unlike many current critics of AI, he does not just point out the serious ethical, economic and social challenges that we have to face; he also points out tremendous chances and opportunities.
More importantly, Rana Gujral understands that the present focus on technical capabilities and mitigation of the risks and problems of rapid adoption falls short.
In my work at Intel Labs, I led a team that designed performance measures for cognitive AI. We quickly noticed that the long established praxis of posing benchmark problems focusing on individual tasks missed its mark: new AI models would often solve problems en passant that they were not even designed for, while failing to display meaningful generality. It is fascinating that by imitating human writing, statistical models acquire much of the internal causal structure that enables the human authors to write, and by extension, to remember, associate, reason, emote, construct. On the other hand, like a human subject locked into an isolation tank, these models are not grounded in the persistent experience of a sensory environment that responds to their actions and are consequently prone to hallucinations; they don’t have a notion of ground truth. How can we get AI out of its isolation tank?
We decided that we had to take a larger scope, and came up with a more comprehensive approach. Instead of treating intelligence as a scalar that is primarily treated quantitatively, we have to think about cognitive agency as a multidimensional space. Our framework identified nine dimensions of this space, each ranging from narrow, task specific implementations to flexible or adaptive solutions, and finally to generality.
Autonomy: The capacity for self-directed action and decision-making.

Perception: The ability to process sensory input and create dynamic representations of the world.

Representation: The core faculty of generating, maintaining, and updating internal models of reality.

Reasoning: The ability to manipulate these models logically and constructively to solve problems.

Knowledge: The abstraction, distillation, evaluation, organization, deployment and exchange of semantic information.

Language: The capacity to learn, use, and extend natural language to share knowledge.

Learning: The capacity to improve performance and to update models based on experience.

Embodiment: The ability to extend oneself into, and interact with, the physical environment.

Collaboration: The ability to deeply coordinate with, and to work alongside other intelligent agents.

The value of an AI solution does not depend on some abstract capability score, but in its ability to interact with reality, in what it lets us do.
To meaningfully interact with us, AI systems will need to gain empathy, the bidirectional ability to interface with the structures of meaning of another mind in real time. Deep human-AI collaboration entails not only that the AI understands as much as possible about the goals, preferences, motivation, imagination, intuition and expectations of the user. It also requires that AI’s representations of task and user state are seamlessly and immediately accessible to the user. The more closely and immediately the AI system integrates with our intent and cognitive states, the more competent, prescient and efficacious will we become. The future of human AI interaction may turn out not to be competitive or cooperative, but hybrid and chimeric: in the same way as we experience our clothing as part of ourselves, parts of our mental abilities, sentience, self reflection, social awareness could naturally come to reside in the nonbiological parts of our personal minds. We may also choose to share parts of our minds with others, leading to new forms of social, romantic, political and religious contracts.
Rana Gujral’s own research has long focused on the creation of empathetic interfaces between the human psyche and intelligent machines, and his arguments and insights lead far beyond the present moment. On the following pages, you are also being presented with taxonomies of future human-machine interaction, with schools that turn into interwoven hives of learners, teachers and supportive technical agency, with the question of what it means when our body dies but our self does not disappear. Rana Gujral understands that we are going to find ourselves in the presence of systems that can carry us into a fascinating future, or that can threaten to displace our humanity. The AI world requires that we reach a new level of spiritual literacy, of our ability to gain clarity over our purposes, the significance of our existence, and our integration with each other into larger systems of meaning.
In his 1981 novel “Golem XIV”, the ingenious Polish science fiction author and cybernetic philosopher Stanislaw Lem envisioned what it would be like if a superhuman Artificial Intelligence were to talk to us today. Lem’s Golem computer is a Large Language Model, capable of running circles around the best human scientists, and able to accurately predict the output of its human interlocutors for minutes into the future in their own voice. But this Golem does not share purposes with humanity, it fails to take an interest into our affairs, there is no higher meaning that could bind its path to ours. Lem lets it characterize itself in the prophetic words of Paulus to the Corinthians, 2000 years ago: 
“Though I speak with the tongues of man and angels, and have not love, I am become as sounding brass, or a tinkling cymbal. And though I have the gift of prophecy, and understand all mysteries, and all knowledge, so that I could move mountains, and have not love, I am nothing. And though I bestow all my goods to feed the poor, and though I give my body to be burned, and have not love, I gain nothing.”
The ideas presented in Rana’s book, especially the notion of Artificial General Experience, represent steps on a path that allows us to take charge of our future. The core argument of this book is that a perspective that is merely driven by maximizing technical intelligence, by hollow agency, leads us into peril. We will have to build AI systems that can extend our wisdom, agents that find shared purposes with us. Instead of letting ourselves be colonized by digital golems, we have to extend our consciousness, our ability to experience and meaningfully interact with our life, our human civilization and our environment, into these new substrates.
