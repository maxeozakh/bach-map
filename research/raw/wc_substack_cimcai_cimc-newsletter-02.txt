Monthly Researcher Note



Written by Ivan Yakovenko
For decades (centuries, really!) we have, reasonably, assumed that creating intelligent machines is a matter of designing the right algorithm. The wall we hit was the unexpected complexity of the problem itself, which led to brittle, unsatisfactory results. What was discovered instead is that we can specify a different algorithm: one that defines how to search for the method that implements intelligence, and let computers do the work. Current advances in AI are the pinnacle of this approach.
Neural Cellular Automata (NCAs) are one possible continuation on this trajectory. They begin with almost no imposed structure, consisting of local rules on a grid, each informationally coupled only to its immediate neighbors. In this way interesting message-passing patterns, and by that the organization of computation itself are discovered rather than designed. This lets us evaluate a wider range of solutions than pre-assumed topologies allow.
Here, the properties (e.g. world modeling, agency, intelligence) we are looking for are emergent from the interactions of identical units across the substrate (the NCA grid). This emergence from simple units, inside-out instead of outside-in, also seems to align with what we see in biological systems.
The engineering work at CIMC involves building systems that let us specify the properties of interest and efficiently run the search, evaluation, and analysis of the resulting automata. Our current hypothesis is that consciousness might be one of those interesting properties, since we observe it as apparently indispensable part in many biological organisms.
The aim is not to declare any particular instance conscious, but to maintain a controllable lab in which the conditions for that kind of organization can be found.


CIMC Hackathon: Lattice Animals
September 25 - San Francisco
A lattice animal is a mathematician’s name for a shape made of connected grid cells. At our September hackathon, each participant wrote the mind for a single cell, and a cell only counted once it found others and held a shape with them. The minds had no way to talk to each other except by where they moved.
Ivan Yakovenko, our principal engineer, built the playground: LatticeAnimals.com, a small code repository so people could plug in AI models running on their own laptops, and an interface for connecting large language models. Ahead of the event he trained a neural cellular automaton to play the game and published it as a demo, so participants could see a working strategy before they started. (A neural cellular automaton is a grid where every cell runs the same small neural network and decides what to do based only on its neighbors.)
Thanks to everyone who came out and built with us. We learned a lot from this one, and the next hackathon will be smoother for it.

A team from the Institute for Advanced Consciousness Studies joined CIMC as grantees in September with their project Dream to Explore: A World-Modeling Architecture for Testing Functional Properties of Consciousness and Multi-Agent Alignment. Led by Zahra Sheikhbahaee, with Adam Safron, Dalton Sakthivadivel, Alex Kiefer, Arthur Juliani and Leonardo Christov-Moore, they’re building AI agents that learn a model of their world, then using them to test ideas about what consciousness does for a mind, such as a “global workspace” where information becomes available to the whole system at once. In their first month, an early prototype was accepted to two NeurIPS workshops, World Models in Physical AI and Continual World Models. Welcome, IACS team!






CIMC Hackathon
October 30 - San Francisco
At CIMC, we’re developing a computational architecture inspired by the visual cortex. Our research brings together self-organizing, distributed systems and action–perception loops, where a system’s perceptions guide its actions, and its actions shape what it perceives next.
The challenge is to build a software architecture that takes video input (from a webcam or virtual world) and predicts the next frame. The goal is a live mirror generating movements in front of the camera, continuously generating what the camera will see next.
It’s an opportunity to experiment with how an architecture represents a changing scene, learns its dynamics, and predicts what comes next.
